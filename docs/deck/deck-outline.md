## Slide-by-slide outline and speaker notes

### Slide 1: TerraVow: Healthy machines. Cleaner factories. Growth that lasts.
*Visual:* Dark title slide with factory silhouette, ring and tagline

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Open with the hook, slowly: everything in this room was made in a factory. Phones, medicines, the clothes we are wearing. Factories are the growth engine of the world, and they are also among its biggest polluters. TerraVow is the bridge between the two. It links the health of a machine to the pollution it creates, tells the operator what to fix and when, and shows greener options with the incentives that can pay for them. We built it on Google Cloud with Gemini, and every number you will see comes from a deterministic engine, not from the model. In the next three minutes we will show the problem, the product running, and why you can trust it.

### Slide 2: Factories grow the world, and they are among its biggest polluters
*Section:* The paradox
*Visual:* Left: growth stat cards; right: native bar chart of 2019 GHG share by sector with industry highlighted

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Here is the paradox. On the left, the growth engine: manufacturing is about 15 percent of world GDP according to the World Bank, 16 to 17 percent of India's GDP according to the Press Information Bureau, and in India small and medium enterprises make about 35 percent of manufacturing output. On the right, the cost. The IPCC says industry produced 24 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2019, and 34 percent once the electricity factories use is attributed to them. That makes it the largest end-use share. And carbon is only one part: factories also release into air, water, chemicals and soil. Caveat for the careful: the World Bank figure is manufacturing only, the IPCC figure is the wider industry sector, so the two shares are not like for like. The point is that both are true, and factories cannot simply switch off.

### Slide 3: The hidden cause: worn machines quietly burn more and leak more
*Section:* The hidden cause
*Visual:* Two native line charts (health falling, effluent rising) + three snag cards + DOE evidence strip

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Here is the part nobody sees. A worn seal lets air in, a drifting burner makes soot, a clogged filter forces the fan to work harder. Each one is a small maintenance snag, and each one makes the machine burn more fuel and release more per tonne of output. On the left is a kiln from our synthetic demo fleet. Over twelve months its health score falls from the high seventies to 28, and its effluent per day climbs from the healthy baseline of 800 cubic metres to about 918. That is an illustrative model, not a measurement, and we say so on every screen. For an outside anchor: the US Department of Energy estimates that operations and maintenance programs aimed at energy efficiency can save 5 to 20 percent on energy bills without significant capital. That is a US federal-sites guide, so read it as direction, not a promise for any one plant.

### Slide 4: Today's tools cannot connect a deferred repair to a pollution event
*Section:* The gap today
*Visual:* Four dark cards (tool category, what it does, blind spot) and a red gap banner

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

So why does this go unseen? Because each team owns one piece. Maintenance systems track work orders and downtime cost, not emissions. ESG and carbon-accounting tools report what you already emitted, using industry-average factors rather than the state of your machine. Compliance and monitoring tools tell you whether a reading is above or below a limit, not why. And a plain chatbot sounds confident but cannot show a calculation you can audit. Nobody connects 'we deferred this repair' to 'we just created avoidable emissions and cost'. And the factory cannot simply stop. Output, jobs and customers depend on it. It needs a practical path that keeps it running while it gets cleaner. These categories come from our competitive review; vendor claims there should be re-verified before quoting.

### Slide 5: TerraVow is the bridge: from machine health to cleaner, funded upgrades
*Section:* The solution
*Visual:* Five-step chevron flow with three principle cards below

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

This is TerraVow in one line of flow. Step one, people on the shop floor report snags against a specific machine model. Step two, a deterministic engine turns that snag history into a health score and an estimate of remaining life. Step three, the health score is linked to pollution intensity across air, water, chemical and soil. Step four, TerraVow recommends greener machines and process changes. Step five, it shows payback with the Indian incentives that may apply, for a small enterprise or a large one, and labels what is unverified. Underneath all of it sit three promises: a safety gate that vetoes anything that adds or merely moves pollution, an audit trail you can verify, and a clean split where Gemini reasons and writes while the engine does the maths.

### Slide 11: Architecture: Gemini for language, a deterministic engine for numbers
*Section:* How it works
*Visual:* Dark boxes architecture diagram with Google services and a 'not in the demo path' strip

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Here is how it fits together, simplified. A single-page app talks to one FastAPI service, designed for Cloud Run. Inside it, a workflow layer handles reporters and resolvers; a deterministic engine computes health, lifespan, pollution intensity and economics; the Decision Gate vets every recommendation; and a cache-once layer decides when Gemini is allowed to be called at all. Gemini 2.5 Flash, through AI Studio or Vertex AI, drafts templates, looks up specs with Google Search grounding, and suggests alternatives. Firestore stores everything, with an in-memory fallback so the demo runs anywhere. Being honest about the Google stack: Gemini, Google Search grounding, Firestore and Cloud Run are what the prototype uses. BigQuery, Vertex AI Search, Agent Engine, Pub/Sub and Cloud Storage are in the full design, and a Java reference engine exists, but they are not in the demo path.

### Slide 12: Technical depth: generate once per model, serve everyone
*Section:* Technical merit
*Visual:* Six-step flow with guard layers and fallback branch

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

This is the technical heart. Generating a deep, model-specific template with Gemini is slow and costs tokens, so it must happen at most once per model, even when many users arrive at once. Step one: read the store; a hit returns immediately. Step two: on a miss, join a single flight, so one request becomes leader and the others wait and share its result. Step three: the leader re-reads the store, a double-checked read. Step four: only now call Gemini, up to three attempts, validating against ten structural rules and sending the errors back as a hint. Step five: create-if-absent is the final guard; if another writer won, ours is discarded and we return theirs. Step six: return generated or cache-hit. If Gemini is unavailable, or the output stays invalid, a deterministic fallback template is returned and never stored, so a bad day cannot poison the cache. Being straight about limits: this guarantees one stored document, but the cross-replica lease is not built yet. Two replicas could both call Gemini; only one result is kept.

**Sources / references:**

- docs/system-architecture.md 'Cache-Once Create-If-Absent Sequence'; docs/user-flow.md 'Template Ensure Cache Sequence'; prototype/app/cache_once.py

### Slide 13: Why AI, and why you can trust it: Gemini reasons, the engine calculates
*Section:* Why AI
*Visual:* Three columns: Gemini | validation | engine, with fallback band

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Why is Gemini essential here, and why can you trust the output? Machine templates differ by model and by industry: a jet dyeing machine and a rotary kiln fail in completely different ways. Nobody can hand-write thousands of those taxonomies, but Gemini can draft one for any model, grounded with Google Search for specs. It also suggests greener alternatives and explains results in plain language. But the model never computes. Everything it returns passes through validation: schema checks, bounds, and a rule that invalid candidates are dropped, never quietly clamped into something else. Gemini gives ranges; the engine takes the midpoint and calculates capex, avoided emissions and payback. On the right, the engine owns health, lifespan, intensity, economics and the gate verdict. And if there is no Gemini key at all, the product degrades to a generic template and curated alternatives, with the same engine and the same gate.

**Sources / references:**

- docs/system-architecture.md 'Deterministic-Numbers-vs-LLM Boundary' and 'Graceful Degradation'; prototype/app/engine.py, gemini_client.py

### Slide 14: Responsible by design: a hard veto that no confidence score can override
*Section:* Trust and safety
*Visual:* Four-stage gate flow with BLOCK/ASK/ALLOW badges and trust checklist

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Safety here is structural, not a disclaimer. Every recommendation passes the Decision Gate in a fixed order. First, a hard veto: if the option would increase total emissions, or merely shift pollution from one medium to another, say from air into water, it is blocked, however confident the model is. Second, confidence: at or above 0.80 allow, at or above 0.60 ask a human, below that block. Third, a trust cap that can only lower a verdict. Fourth, an audit entry is written to a hash-chained log before the response returns. In the demo, nothing reaches Allow: AI-suggested options carry confidence 0.55 and curated ones 0.70, so a person always confirms. Around the gate: estimate and illustrative labels everywhere, personal data stripped from free text, vendor validation required for AI suggestions, and plain honesty that the demo has no authentication.

**Sources / references:**

- docs/overview.md 'Decision Gate'; docs/user-flow.md Sustainability journey; prototype/app/gate.py; prototype/README.md

### Slide 15: Not another CMMS, ESG ledger or chatbot: TerraVow connects the dots
*Section:* Differentiation
*Visual:* Capability matrix with filled/half/empty markers for 5 categories vs TerraVow

How are we different? We assessed five neighbouring categories. Predictive-maintenance and CMMS tools know machine health but do not price pollution or recommend greener alternatives. TerraVow combines per-machine health, pollution linkage, greener options with payback, Indian incentive adjustment, deterministic auditable numbers, and a hard veto in one flow. ### Slide 16: Illustrative impact: one Critical kiln, and what each fix would be worth
*Section:* Impact (illustrative)

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

A worked example from the synthetic demo fleet, so treat every number as illustrative. Kiln K-4 has fallen to a health of 28.4, Critical, with roughly 342 days of estimated life left. Against its healthy reference it is running about 14.8 percent higher on effluent, around 118 cubic metres a day, and 5.4 percent higher on CO2e, roughly 2.3 tonnes a day. ### Slide 22: Appendix B | Data model: nine collections, one audit chain
*Section:* Appendix
*Visual:* Native 3x3 grid of collection cards with relationship arrows

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Backup slide on data. Nine collections hold everything. Industries and the sample catalogue are reference data. Templates and alternatives are the cached AI outputs, and both refuse to store any document whose source is not AI, so a fallback can never poison the cache. ### Slide 23: Appendix C | The four user flows, step by step
*Section:* Appendix

**Speaker notes (30-60 s):**

Backup slide with the four flows from the repo's user-flow document, condensed into lanes.
